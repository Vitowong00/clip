## Tom Sasse, our South Asia bureau chief, on the bleak future facing the few Afghan girls still able to study

Down a dusty side street in Kabul we came to a gate. Two security guards quickly invited us in. Behind it was a ramshackle three-storey building, with a small outdoor area covered in green astro-turf. We went inside and sat a while in a reception room, where everyone seemed nervous. A few phone calls were made. And then four girls were brought in—Farishta, Diwa, Asia and Nadia. They said they would be happy to tell me about being a schoolgirl in Afghanistan.  

我们沿着喀布尔一条尘土飞扬的小巷来到一扇门前。两名保安迅速请我们进去。门后是一栋破旧的三层楼房，带有一个铺着绿色人造草坪的小型户外区域。我们进去后在接待室里坐了一会儿，每个人似乎都很紧张。有人打了几通电话。然后，四个女孩——法丽什塔、迪瓦、阿西亚和娜迪亚——被带了进来。她们说很乐意跟我分享她们在阿富汗当学生的经历。

I was there because I was reporting this week’s cover story, on Afghanistan, five years after the Taliban returned to power, which will be published on Thursday. We will also be devoting this week’s episode of the [Insider](https://click.e.economist.com/?qs=ABB7InYiOjEsImQiOjQ5NjV9AAcAAAAABR1hJffxteszIl1aXGHp8WqyY-ic_jWZw_Ss5fq9WPRafnjgjvj2zftMISSz9C_B15m-OopaqgxQbrm9lYNUYh03glstFo9oM9uZfw8) to how the militants are ruling the country a second time around, and how the world should respond. But in this newsletter, I wanted to give space to the voices of these Afghan girls.  

我当时在那里，是因为我要报道本周的封面故事，主题是塔利班重返阿富汗五年后的现状，这篇报道将于周四发表。本周的《内幕》节目也将专门探讨武装分子如何再次统治这个国家，以及国际社会应该如何应对。但在这篇简报中，我想让这些阿富汗女孩的声音被更多人听到。

They were all 16 or 17, and dressed in dark black robes. Asia was the only one wearing a full burqa, which covers everything but the eyes. The others wore abayas, purple headscarves and covid-style masks over their mouths (this has become the most common way for Afghan women who don’t wear burqas to indicate that they are complying with the Taliban’s dress codes). I began the interview slowly in English, unsure whether they would need my translator. “We understand you perfectly,” Nadia politely corrected me.  

她们都只有十六七岁，身穿黑色长袍。只有阿西亚穿着全套罩袍，除了眼睛以外，全身都被遮盖住了。其他人都穿着长袍，戴着紫色头巾，嘴上戴着类似新冠疫情期间使用的口罩（这已成为不穿罩袍的阿富汗女性最常见的表示她们遵守塔利班着装规定的方式）。我用英语慢慢地开始采访，不确定她们是否需要我的翻译。“我们完全听懂了，”娜迪亚礼貌地纠正道。

They were all delighted to be back in school, but they were the lucky ones, Farishta said. They had lost several years of education after the Taliban [closed government secondary schools](https://click.e.economist.com/?qs=ABB7InYiOjEsImQiOjQ5NjV9AAcAAAAABR1hJffyrqD5zttGevOO7eIQBWCrgS8H5N0oyGifZlDfh37D7Lo1GcTTKrN74ww80Rw8DAkyL0G0OqO-eK4GqORxZjz0kR0m_iDa_nE) to girls in 2021. The _madrasa_ (Islamic school) I was visiting opened two years ago and they and their families hustled to pay the fees, which were around 1,000 afghanis ($15) a month. Lots of their friends could not afford them. There is no reliable figure, but experts I asked guessed that perhaps as little as 10% of secondary-age girls in Afghanistan are getting a formal education.  

法里什塔说，她们都很高兴能重返校园，但她们只是幸运儿。2021年塔利班关闭了政府中学，禁止女生入学，她们因此失去了好几年的教育。我参观的这所伊斯兰学校两年前开办，她们和家人为了支付每月约1000阿富汗尼（约合15美元）的学费而奔波劳碌。她们的很多朋友都负担不起。虽然没有确切的数字，但我咨询过的专家估计，阿富汗适龄女学生中，接受正规教育的比例可能只有10%左右。

As with many aspects of life in Afghanistan, _madrasas_ operate in a grey area. Officially, secondary schooling for girls has been suspended by the country’s hardline emir. But that policy is hugely unpopular with Afghans—and even, in fact, among the ruling Talibs. The workaround is that girls can attend privately run _madrasas_ as long as, in theory, they only get a religious education. Of course, parents, teachers and some Talib governors want them to learn other things. But it has to happen quietly, or else there will be a crackdown.

All four girls at the _madrasa_ I visited said that they enjoyed doing Islamic subjects because they genuinely liked learning about their religion (they also said that they had no problem with codes requiring women to dress conservatively, which they anyway considered part of their culture). But they also got to do many other subjects, including maths, science and English, and all had ambitions to keep studying and learning more. Diwa said she was paying the fees by teaching other girls English online in her spare time.

When I got a tour later I could see that their school was a hastily converted house, with peeling wallpaper and moth-eaten carpets. The principal (who asked not to be identified) said he was educating some 600 girls there in the small, cramped classrooms. Money was short, because many pupils could not afford the full fees and the school’s one foreign sponsor had cancelled its donation. It was hard to find maths and English teachers. Still, the girls said that the education they were getting there was good, better in fact than at the government-run schools they went to before. And at least now, because Kabul is not a war zone, they could walk to school safely.

The problem was that even though they had fought to get this precarious education, they wouldn’t be able to use it because they were barred from most jobs and university courses. Asia said she had dreams of being a lawyer but she knew that would not happen. Nadia had wanted to be a doctor. They saw the only option as becoming a teacher in an Islamic school, which they didn’t see as much of a future.

They were angry that Afghan men hadn’t done more for them. “They haven’t raised their voices, they haven’t fought for our rights,” said Nadia. Asia agreed: “It’s not just the Taliban, it’s the mindset of men—they don’t want women to be equal, that’s the truth.” They wanted the world not to forget about them, and thought that other countries should recognise their government, engage with it, and keep pushing it to give them more opportunities.

But they were not optimistic. Diwa said that her parents kept telling her that by the time she graduated, things would be OK. “But I don’t think it will be OK,” she said. “I don’t hope for any future.”
